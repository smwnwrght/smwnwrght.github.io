*There are two methods for calculating the age of the universe. Not only do they disagree, the disagreement is growing.*

The disagreement comes down to one thing that it’s hard to do in space: measure distance.

Finding the age of the universe requires building a model of how the universe evolved over time. 

When we look into space we can see stars, supernovae, galaxies, clusters of galaxies, black holes, exoplanets and so on. And because light doesn’t travel instantly, the further into space we look, the further back in time we can see.

But what does it mean to look “deep into space” if you can’t measure how far away something is?

# The short history of the long history of the Universe

In 1908 American astronomer Henrietta Leavitt discovered variable stars that were brighter on some photographs and fainter on others taken at different times. She noticed that the brighter the variable star, the longer its period.

The 16 variable stars Leavitt measured were all in the same group of stars, the Small Magellanic Cloud. Thus they were all approximately the same distance from the Earth.

Her conclusion was a remarkable *"period-luminosity relation"* — the longer the period, or time, from a Cepheid's maximum brightness to minimum and back to maximum, the greater the intrinsic luminosity of the star.

!image.png

*Image: Plot from a paper prepared by Leavitt in 1912. Magnitude vs log period of 25 cepheids. There are two trend lines showing the maximum and minimum brightness for each Cepheid.*

https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1912HarCi.173....1L

Astronomers Ejnar Hertzsprung and Harlow Shapley took this one step further by estimating how far various cepheids were from Earth, using geometric methods like stellar parallax.

This provided a calibration step that allows us to infer the distance to any cepheid. With this calibration you can measure its period to work out its inherent (actual) luminosity, and the distance can be inferred by comparing the inherent and apparent luminosity (further cepheids appear dimmer to us).

By 1928 astronomers had discovered that different objects in space appeared to be moving at different velocities away from us. This was easy to measure in the redshift of the light from those nebulae.

Using the 100-inch Hooker Telescope, the world’s largest at the time, Edwin Hubble surveyed the night sky for several much dimmer cepheid variables. The further from the Earth they are, the dimmer they appear to us.

Hubble directed his assistant Milton Humason to measured the red-shift in the spectra of light from their galaxies. And in 1929 paper, Hubble published a paper which provided evidence of a very important relation: that the furthest objects were moving the fastest.

!image.png

We now know this relation between velocity and distance as the Hubble Constant, H₀.

<aside>
💥

$H_0 = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{redshift}}{\text{distance}}$

Note: this relationship is an approximation that works for short distances. Over longer distances you need to take into account that the expansion rate changes.

</aside>

It was immediately clear that the universe is expanding. H0 is a measurement of how fast this expansion is happening, and tracing this expansion backwards in time predicts that the universe had almost zero size at a finite time in the past. This concept, initially known as the "Primeval Atom" by Lemaitre, was later elaborated into the modern Big Bang theory.

Hubble's measurements on 46 galaxies lead to a value for this expansion, H0 of 500 kilometres per second per megaparsec (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC522427/). 

That number was a problem even then. A crude estimation of the age of the universe, assuming the expansion has always been at the same rate, gives a “Hubble time”, 1/H₀ of about 2 billion years. 

Radioactive dating of rocks already showed Earth was older than 2 billion years (estimates at the time were over 3 billion).

Hubble had used Hertzsprung and Shapley’s geometric calibration to estimate the distance of very dim cepheids from their apparent luminosity. But he had mistaken bright star-forming regions for individual stars, and so had underestimated how far away galaxies were, leading to an over-estimate of the Hubble Constant.

# Stairway to heaven

What Hubble had built is now called a ‘distance ladder’. This principle uses a geometry to relate distance to brightness of some object, so that you can look further into space and use the brightness of the same type of object to estimate its distance.

These objects with a reliable absolute brightness are known as ‘standard candles’. And Hubble’s huge over-estimate of H0 shows how a mistake in a lower rung can propagate up the ladder.

The SH0ES (Supernova H₀ for the Equation of State) team, an astronomical research project led by Nobel laureate Adam Riess, was established with the primary goal of measuring the Hubble constant.

Modern distance ladders can be built from various combinations of ‘rungs’. The best chance of an accurate measurement of H0 is with a very long ladder, reaching as far as possible into space.

!image.png

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_distance_ladder#/media/File:Extragalactic_Distance_Ladder_en.svg

!image.png

https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O26007/snakes-and-ladders-board-game/?carousel-image=2016JF1244

The distance ladder of choice for the SH0ES group leads to a type of supernova. Supernovae are very bright so can be detected very far into space, and Type 1a supernovae are very good standard candles in that they have a inherent luminosity that is reliably calculable.

## **Rung 1: Find the relationship between a Cepheid’s period and its luminosity.**

As we saw earlier, Levitt’s discovery of cepheid variables as pulsating stars, means we know the length of time between pulses, their period, is related to their size and brightness. The period of a Cepheid can be as little as a day to around 50 days.

These distance can be measured by observing how star clusters that include cepheids move across the sky in a parallax effect as the Earth rotates around the Sun. The star clusters used are normally quite close to us — usually within the Milky Way. The distance of these star clusters from Earth can then be estimated trigonometrically.

!image.png

Image: ESA

Once the distance is known scientists can use the observed luminosity of the Cepheid to estimate its inherent luminosity. This allows a mathematical relationship to be established between a Cepheid’s period and its luminosity.

## **Rung 2: Use the period-distance relationship to calibrate the brightness of Type Ia supernovae.**

Using the mathematical relationship established in the first rung between a Cepheid’s period and its luminosity, various other Cepheids from deeper into space can be analysed. The period of each Cepheid can be used to find its inherent brightness, and its observed brightness can then be used to estimate its distance from Earth. This is the same method that Hubble used.

A Type Ia supernova is the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf in a binary system. After taking into account their width and colour, Type 1a supernovae are good standard candles, i.e. always approximately the same inherent luminosity.

!image.png

*Image: Hubble Space Telescope images of galaxies that are hosts to both Cepheid variables and Type 1a supernovae. Credit: NASA, ESA, Adam G. Riess (STScI, JHU) https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-constant-and-tension/*

To make the link between cepheids and supernovae, about 40 galaxies have been identified that host both a well-observed Type Ia supernova and Cepheids. At this rung, we can calculate the distance of the galaxy using the Cepheids, and use that distance along with the observed luminosity to calibrate the inherent luminosity of Type 1a supernovae.

## **Rung 3: Find far-flung Type 1a supernovae and measure their distance and speed to calculate the Hubble Constant.**

Using the knowledge of a Type 1a supernova’s inherent luminosity, it is possible to estimate the distance of several examples from much further away —  beyond the limits of detection of Cepheid stars.

And finally, by comparing the distance of the supernovae with their speed calculated from redshift, scientists can do what Hubble did and make new estimates of the Hubble Constant.

!image.png

*Image: The SH0ES distance ladder. Green: geometry calibrates cepheid magnitude. Blue: Cepheid magnitude calibrates SN1a magnitude. Red: SN1a magnitude calibrates SN1a distance. The distance-redshift relation provides H0. Credit: Reiss 2024*

The calculation of the Hubble constant, as before, is:

<aside>
💥

$H_0 = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{redshift}}{\text{distance}}$

</aside>

Probably the most important recent distance ladder H0 determination published by the SH0ES collaboration was in 2022, using Hubble Space Telescope and JWST observations to calibrate 42 Type 1a supernovae, giving a Hubble Constant of 73.04±1.04 km/s/Mpc.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04510

(A 2025 update adding additional JWST observations found H₀ = 73.49 ± 0.93 km/s/Mpc)

https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01667

# Cosmic microwave background

The CMB was discovered by accident in 1964 at Bell Labs using a horn antenna built to detect radio waves bounced off Echo balloon satellites. Their attempts to remove all interference from their receiver included cooling the receiver to 4 Kelvin with liquid helium and famously removing some pigeons and their accumulated droppings from the antenna.

!image.png

*Image:* The Holmdel Horn Antenna on which Penzias and Wilson discovered the cosmic microwave background.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background#/media/File:Horn_Antenna-in_Holmdel,_New_Jersey_-_restoration1.jpg

!image.png

*A pigeon trap, on view at the Air and Space Museum, used by Nobel Prize winners Penzias and Wilson to remove the birds roosting in the radio antenna's large horn. NASM/SI, Loan from Robert Wilson*

https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/how-scientists-confirmed-big-bang-theory-owe-it-all-to-a-pigeon-trap-180949741/

But no matter what they did they couldn’t suppress a low, steady, mysterious noise, present day and night, evenly spread over the sky.

And this light can provide another way to measure distances in the sky, infer the Hubble Constant, and estimate the age of the universe.

!image.png

*Image: CMB measured by the Planck spacecraft* 

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background#/media/File:Cosmic_Microwave_Background_(CMB).jpeg.jpeg)

The CMB is the oldest light in the universe, released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. These photons were released from the slurry of hot dense fog of photons and matter, at the time when atoms first began to form in a process called ‘recombination’. The photons released at recombination are still the vast majority of photons in the universe, outnumbering other photons by around 400 to one.

As a result, this light is a snapshot of recombination, when those photons were released from the plasma. Within the CMB, very slight variations — of roughly one part in 100,000 — have been caused by spherical ripples of sound that were emanating through the plasma before recombination.

These ripples had 380,000 years to travel, until they suddenly stopped. And because we know the composition of the CMB, we can work out how fast the ripples were expanding over that 380,000 years, giving a fixed radius for the sound horizon of 0.14 Mpc. This pattern in the CMB has been mapped in detail by satellites like COBE, WMAP, and Planck.

The CMB temperature was about 2892 K at the drag epoch, compared with 2.7255 K today — a stretch of roughly 1,061. At the time the BAO sound horizon was set it was about 0.139 Mpc, corresponding to about 147 Mpc today.

https://pdg.lbl.gov/2023/reviews/rpp2023-rev-cosmic-microwave-background.pdf

image: CMB blackbody shift

And those ripples have been detected. Looking deep into space, cosmologists can find statistical evidence that pairs of galaxies are more likely to be found at a certain distance ‘r’. In other words, the pattern of ripples present at the time of recombination *has* persisted in the matter of the universe. These ripples are called Baryon Acoustic Oscillations, BAO, and they are a very useful in constraining the value of H0 using the CMB.

!image.png

*Image: This illustration shows a series of galaxies distributed throughout space, with many galaxies distributed in a spherical structure that represents an oscillatory feature imprinted in the very early Universe. These baryon acoustic oscillations, seen statistically for a few decades now, have for the first time been identified in an individual structure: Ho`oleilana. An illustration of clustering patterns due to Baryon Acoustic Oscillations, where the likelihood of finding a galaxy at a certain distance from any other galaxy is governed by the relationship between dark matter and normal matter, as well as the effects of normal matter as it interacts with radiation. As the Universe expands, this characteristic distance expands as well, allowing us to measure the Hubble constant, the dark matter density, and even the scalar spectral index. The results agree with the CMB data, and a Universe made up of ~25% dark matter, as opposed to 5% normal matter, with an expansion rate of around 67 km/s/Mpc.* 

Credit: Zosia Rostomian, LBNL

!image.png

*Image: The bump in the middle of the correlation function shows that galaxy pairs have an increased likelihood to be found at a certain distance ‘r’. This distance is the radius of the sound horizon with expansion taken into account, which is determined from the temperature change of the CMB since recombination.*

Astrophysicists can use this data to build a 3D map of the universe. Two measurements are taken for thousands of galaxies:

1. the angular position
2. their redshifts

Using these in combination it is possible to then build up a 3D map which maximises the correlation of galaxy pairs being at the expanded sound horizon distance 147 Mpc*.

!image.png

*Image: The BAO sound horizon scale can be thought of as a ‘standard ruler’.* *Although the light from the galaxy surveys is from an earlier, less expanded universe as distance increases, the 3D map plots the bias as being 147 Mpc, so the galaxies are located on the map ‘where they are today’. This idea is called using comoving units. Credit: Ángel López-Sánchez, Australian Astronomical Observatory/Macquarie University.*

From this 3D plot of galaxies we can compare how far they are to their redshift, once again providing us with terms for Hubble’s equation.

<aside>
💥

$H_0 = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{redshift}}{\text{distance}}$

</aside>

However, this method looks so deep into space that we are measuring the history of expansion H(z), rather than the local rate of expansion H0.

H(z) is a function which relates the rate of expansion H to redshift z.

Which we will modify slightly to measure the Hubble parameter for the where the bubble is by looking at how the redshift changes over distance.

<aside>
💫

$H(z) = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{change in redshift}}{\text{change in distance}}$

</aside>

A final analysis of data from the Planck cosmology probe’s was released in July 2018, providing the most precise measurements of the parameters of the standard ΛCDM cosmological model, and determines the Hubble constant to be 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc.

https://doi.org/10.1051%2F0004-6361%2F201936386

# The uncertain expansion

These two methods — cosmic distance ladders and cosmic background radiation — both declare a Hubble Constant with high confidence. 

**Cosmic distance ladder**

73.04 ± 1.04 km/s/Mpc

(SH0ES 2022)

**Cosmic microwave background**

67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc

(Planck 2018)

The only problem is, they disagree with each other. Each of them reports an error range much smaller than the difference between the two. These disagreeing measurements form part of the debate amongst cosmologists which is known as the *Hubble Tension*. 

And it’s getting worse: the more precise our measurements of either the local or CMB, the more narrowly constrained the measurements get, and the less they look like they agree.

!image.png

*Image: Disagreement increases with time. Credit: Perivolaropoulos and Skara* 

And these values are repeatable: direct measurements tend to clump around 73 km/s/Mpc, and surveys of the early universe tend to clump around 67 km/s/Mpc.

# The uncertain age of the universe

The CMB is an early universe measurement which requires a prediction of how the universe expands over time, in order to play the tape forwards and find H0.

On the other hand, the distance ladder provides a direct measurement of H0, and so to find the age of the universe you need to assume a model, to play the tape backwards.

In particular the CMB provides a lot of information about the composition of the early universe that supports a model of expansion called ΛCDM. This model provides equations of the shape of the expansion over time.

BAO measurements constrain the scale of that expansion shape by matching it to measurements of H(z), leading to an estimate of H0 and the age of the universe. The distance ladder provides a measurement of H0 which allows you to calculate the age of the universe.

The two methods disagree on H0 by around 8%, and this propagates to their respective prediction of the age of the universe

# Where next?

The basic Hubble relation is deceptively simple: compare the redshift to the distance, and you’ll have it. But cosmic distances are hard to measure.

Most proposals for solving the tension now focus on altering the calibration of either the standard candles (SNIa) or the standard rulers (BAOs).

Perhaps the distance ladder is wrong: maybe the cepheids or SN1as are a ‘bad batch’ with unusual luminosities or biased redshifts?

Or could the BAO scale be wrong: maybe there is some unusual physics which means we’ve miscalculated the size of the sound horizon at the drag epoch (reducing it by 10 Mpc for example would lead to a value of H0 matching the SH0ES data).

However, there’s a third way: the link between the early and late universe measurements — the ΛCDM model — could be the problem. This is the most exciting prospect for physicists because it’d mean ‘new physics’.

Things like *sterile neutrinos*, *dark radiation*, or *early dark energy* could all potentially shift H0, and arguing about these hypotheticals helps to keep the physicists busy.

# *Addendum: a single BAO sphere

The BAO method is fundamentally statistical, in that it deals with a bias rather than individual spheres. In other words the pattern is more like the ripples from a handful of gravel hitting a pond than lots of single pebbles.

However I think it is quite instructive to consider what this methodology involves in terms of a single spherical BAO ‘bubble’ at some depth in space. 

What can we find out about it?

<aside>
🌟

- **radius**: we know from the CMB that its radius is equivalent to the sound horizon, 150 Mpc
- **redshift**: we can measure its redshift from us
- **depth**: we can measure its depth in terms of the gap in redshift from the nearest to the farthest part of the bubble
- **width**: we can measure its width as a number of degrees it takes up in the sky
</aside>

#### Depth — along the line of sight

We can measure the sphere’s depth in terms of redshift.

Now we can use the same equation used by Hubble:

<aside>
💥

$H_0 = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{redshift}}{\text{distance}}$

</aside>

Which we will modify slightly to measure the Hubble parameter for the where the bubble is (at its redshift).

<aside>
💫

$H(z) = \frac{\text{speed of light}\:\times\: \text{redshift gap}}{\text{diameter}}$

</aside>

So the rate of expansion H(z) at the redshift z of the bubble can be found from the speed of light, the difference in redshift and the diameter (2 x radius of 150 Mpc).

### Width — perpendicular to the line of sight

Across the line of sight we can also measure the width of the bubble.

This measurement itself is not a distance but the angle it spans across the sky. Basic trigonometry allows you to find the ratio of the width of the bubble to the distance it is from us. Combine that with the knowledge that the radius of the bubble is 150 Mpc, it is possible to determine its distance from us.

### Creating a cosmological model

In reality individual bubbles are not easy to identify — each ripple interacts with lots of others, like the gravel on a lake.

These two independent measurements, and the requirement that the BAO scale be the same across and along the line of sight, provides a way to validate a 3D map of galaxies, checking that their positions and redshifts make sense.

These maps are typically produced as a result of cosmological models, it gives a way to validate those models.
